Q: Gear teeth are designed to be strongest when tooth contact occurs:
+ where the pitch line and the centreline intersect
- at the tip of the tooth, where leverage is lowest
- down at the root fillet, where the tooth is thickest
- at the heel (large end) of the tooth
E: Maximum strength is where the pitch line and centreline intersect. The further the contact moves from that point (toward heel, toe, up the face or down the flank), the weaker the tooth and the more likely it is to be damaged.

Q: A gear tooth wear pattern that has moved down the flank toward the root fillet indicates:
+ an increased chance of gear tooth damage
- a normal break-in pattern
- correct backlash for an off-road gear set
- reduced load on the tooth
E: Any time the pattern drifts toward the heel, toe, up the face or down the flank, contact is moving away from the strongest point (pitch line / centreline intersection) and the risk of tooth damage increases.

Q: Insufficient clearance between the tip of one gear's tooth and the root of the mating gear will most likely cause:
+ poor lubrication, and damage as the gears heat and expand
- excessive backlash, causing gear rattle under light load
- added axial thrust on the shaft support bearings
- quieter operation with less tooth wear over time
E: Tip-to-root clearance is needed for lubrication and for heat expansion. With too little clearance, the gears expand under torque and heat and damage occurs.

- Rotate both gears together under load and read the dial indicator
- Load the gear set and measure the gap on the drive side of the tooth
- Move both gears in opposite directions and halve the reading
E: Backlash is measured with no load: one gear is held and the other is moved. It doesn't matter which one, but only one moves.

Q: Why are helical gears quieter and stronger than spur gears?
+ Multiple teeth are always in contact, and the angled teeth are longer
- They have fewer teeth, so fewer meshing impacts
- They produce no thrust load, so bearings run cooler
- They run with zero backlash, so the teeth can't hammer
E: Helical teeth overlap, so more than one tooth is always in contact (quieter), and the angled tooth is longer (stronger).

E: Single helical gears produce axial (end) thrust under load, as well as radial thrust, so bearings must handle the end loads.

E: The opposite-angle halves create equal and opposite axial thrust, which cancels. Radial thrust is still present.
